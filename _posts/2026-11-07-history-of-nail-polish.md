A fresh coat of polish feels thoroughly modern. In fact, people have been colouring their nails for thousands of years, often as a sign of status.

## Ancient times: colour as rank

Historians trace nail colouring back roughly 5,000 years. Accounts of ancient Babylon describe men tinting their nails with kohl, with the colour signalling social class, and a manicure set made of solid gold.

In ancient China, nail colour was also tied to status. During the Ming dynasty, lacquers were made from **beeswax, egg whites, gelatin, vegetable dyes and gum arabic**, and the most prestigious shades were reserved for the elite. In ancient Egypt, upper-class women stained their nails with henna.

## The 1920s: a lesson from the car industry

The modern polish story starts with automobiles. In the 1920s, makeup artist **Michelle Ménard** took inspiration from the glossy enamel used on cars and adapted it for nails. Flappers loved it, and wore it in the "moon manicure", painting the middle of the nail and leaving the tip and the half-moon at the base bare.

Brands followed quickly. Cutex and Glazo offered a rose-coloured lacquer in 1924, and in **1932 Revlon** launched an opaque, long-lasting enamel in pinks and reds that brought polish to a mass market.

## Mid-century glamour and the French manicure

Polish became part of Hollywood glamour, often matched with lipstick. Revlon's 1950s "Fire and Ice" campaign turned a red polish into a cultural moment. Then, at the Paris fashion shows of 1978, a natural pink base with white tips caught on so strongly that it became known as the **French manicure**, still one of the most requested looks today.

## Today: gel, art and self-expression

The 1980s brought professional nail lines and new techniques, and gel polish later changed the game with its instant curing and weeks-long wear. Today, a manicure can be anything from a sheer, healthy-looking finish to detailed nail art. After 5,000 years, the ritual is still about the same thing: feeling put-together and expressing who you are.
